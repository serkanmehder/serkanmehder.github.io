## Submitted/Working Papers

### The Effects of Price Transparency on Vacation Rentals: Evidence from California's Honest Pricing Law

(joint work with Maxime Cohen, Armin Khamoshi and Ovunc Yilmaz)

*(Paper complete — awaiting Expedia Group’s permission to submit.)*

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Abstract</summary>

Price transparency regulations are becoming increasingly common worldwide, yet the way they affect sellers and buyers on two-sided platforms remains largely unknown. We study the impact of California’s Honest Pricing Law (HPL), which requires vacation rental platforms to display fee-inclusive prices when travelers compare alternative listings. Using proprietary data from Vrbo, we examine host pricing decisions as well as traveler search and booking behavior. Applying a difference-in-differences design, we first show that the HPL led California hosts to reduce cleaning fees by 14.6% and increase base nightly prices by 2.9%, indicating that hosts adjusted both price components. We then examine heterogeneity in this response. Cleaning fee reductions are larger and base nightly price increases are smaller in more competitive local markets, as proxied by listing density. Professional hosts respond mainly by reducing cleaning fees while non-professional hosts respond by increasing base nightly prices, narrowing the gap in total nightly prices between the two host types. Using a synthetic difference-in-differences design, we then examine traveler demand and find no significant decline in booking conversion for California listings, which were directly targeted by the HPL. By contrast, booking conversion declines for non-California listings viewed by California-located travelers, where the HPL affects price display only for those travelers and therefore creates weaker adjustment incentives for hosts. These findings highlight host price adjustment as an important factor shaping traveler responses to price-transparency regulations.

### [The Effects of Within-Session Price Increases on Consumer Behavior: Evidence from Field Experiments](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7508619)

(joint with Domenico Fabrizi, Jiankun Sun, Ovunc Yilmaz and Zifeng Zhao)

*Under review, Manufacturing & Service Operations Management*

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Abstract</summary>

**Problem Definition.** Online consumers can select a product or service at one price, only to encounter a higher price before purchase. In online retail, cart contents can generate such increases through promotional rules or seller assignment. We examine how these within-cart price increases affect shopping and purchasing behavior across carts and over time, and when platforms should absorb them by preserving earlier displayed prices at their own expense. **Methodology/Results.** We conduct three randomized field experiments with 1000Farmacie, an Italian online pharmacy marketplace, combining purchase outcomes with event-level clickstream data. Relative to absorption, pass-through reduces new-customer conversion by 9.95 percentage points and lowers expenditure. Conversion losses are greater for lower-value carts, particularly at larger price increases. Clickstream data show that pass-through increases initial removals of affected products, with much of the conversion loss involving customers who first remove a product and then abandon the purchase within minutes. Beyond the focal purchase, pass-through reduces subsequent purchasing over 90 days. Among recurring customers, conversion also falls, but less so for those who previously completed a purchase involving a within-cart price increase. **Managerial Implications.** We develop a selective-absorption framework, now implemented at 1000Farmacie, that uses initial cart information to determine which increases to absorb. The fitted rule maximizes predicted profit, including future customer value. In an out-of-sample experiment, the rule yields higher estimated profit and revenue than always passing through and higher estimated profit than always absorbing. We extend the framework to generate policies that trade some profit for additional revenue, allowing management to select the extent of absorption according to its profitability and growth objectives.
